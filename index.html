Job Market Paper

Reclaiming Neighborhoods: Violent Crime and Business Growth

Paper (coming soon)

One in four firms in Latin America identifies crime as a major constraint on its operations. Can reducing violence help businesses grow? I study Rio de Janeiro’s Pacifying Police Units, one of the largest policing interventions in the developing world, which sharply reduced violence in some of the city’s most violent neighbourhoods. Linking its staggered rollout to geocoded administrative records for formal establishments and workers, I compare firms in earlier- and later-treated areas. Four years after treatment, incumbent firms employed 24.2 percent more workers and paid 26.2 percent higher wage bills. Gains were concentrated among firms with fewer than five workers, and firm entry also rose. Customer-facing firms show no clear advantage, offering little support for customer inflows as the main explanation. Among small incumbents, higher-paying firms expand more, new hires are more educated and experienced, and wages within firms show no detectable increase. Under benchmark assumptions, a model of local labour markets attributes most of the small-firm employment response to improved access to workers, with a smaller contribution from reduced crime-related production costs. These findings suggest that the reduction of violent crime can help small firms grow by widening the pool of workers willing to work in previously unsafe areas.

Working Papers

Call It Femicide: Legal Recognition, Victim Protection, and Domestic Violence(submitted)CEP Discussion Paper 2177 | CESifo Working Paper 12956

with Rui Costa, Olivia Masi, and Matteo Sandi

Paper

Can criminal law prevent domestic violence and change how victims use the state? We study Brazil’s 2015 recognition of femicide as qualified homicide using ten years of linked population, police, hospital, and prison records for 2.6 million women. Exploiting predetermined municipal exposure to men with histories of violence against women, we find that police-recorded non-fatal domestic violence fell disproportionately in more exposed areas. Conditional on an incident reaching police, requests for protective measures rose, while pursuit of charges did not. Legal recognition at the lethal endpoint can therefore affect both violence below that margin and survivors’ choice of state remedy.
